2. Clerks. Factory workers. Housemaids. All holding cash.

6. So how did one small man in a straw hat fool an entire city?
7. Charles Ponzi stepped off a ship in Boston in 1903.
8. By his own account, he had two dollars and fifty cents left.
9. He drifted from job to job. Then north, to Canada. And into a prison cell, for forgery.
10. Remember that cell. Later, a newspaper will find it.
11. By 1919, he was back in Boston. Charming. Broke. And about to open a very lucky letter.
12. Inside was a little slip of paper. An international reply coupon.
13. Created in 1906, it let you prepay someone's reply postage, anywhere in the world.
14. But after the war, money in Europe lost value. The coupon's price didn't change.
15. So buy coupons cheap in Italy. Trade them in America for more stamps.
16. On paper, it was legal. Here's the catch: you can't pay rent with stamps.
17. He never explained it. People invested anyway.
18. January 1920. The Securities Exchange Company opens its doors.
19. The first investors really did get paid. With money from the next investors.
20. Then those, with the money from the next. It only works while the line keeps growing.
21. By the end of July, nearly ten million dollars had poured in.
22. He bought a mansion. And a big stake in a Boston bank.
23. Then the crowd got nervous. And came back for their money.
24. Ponzi paid them, smiling. But what nobody knew was who would sink him.
25. Not the police. His own publicist.
26. A man he had hired just ten days earlier.
27. William McMasters was hired to make Ponzi look good. He looked at the books instead.
28. On August 2nd, the Boston Post called Ponzi hopelessly insolvent.
29. The math was brutal. His plan needed 160 million coupons. About 27,000 existed.
30. Then the paper found that old prison cell in Canada.
31. Within days, Ponzi was arrested. His bank failed. Other banks went down with it.
32. Investors lost an estimated twenty million dollars. Many got back less than thirty cents on the dollar.
33. Ponzi pleaded guilty to mail fraud. The newspaper won a Pulitzer. He won five years.
34. He was deported to Italy, and died in 1949 in a charity ward in Rio. He left seventy-five dollars.
35. That was his ending. Here's where this reaches your money.
36. In 2009, Bernie Madoff pleaded guilty to the biggest Ponzi scheme in history. His statements showed investors about sixty-five billion dollars. Most of it never existed.
37. And in 2024 alone, Americans told the FBI they lost over six and a half billion dollars to investment fraud.
38. The costume changes. Crypto apps. Trading groups. A friend's can't-lose fund. The trick doesn't.
39. The SEC's red flags. Guaranteed high returns with little risk. Returns that never go down. A secret strategy nobody can explain. A seller who isn't registered. Trouble getting your money out.
40. Before you invest a dollar, look them up at investor dot gov.
41. A century later, the line is still there. It just moved onto your phone.
42. Fifty percent in forty-five days. Would you have stood in that line?
